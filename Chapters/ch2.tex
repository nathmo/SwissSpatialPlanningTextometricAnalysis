\section{Conceptual Framework \& Key Definitions}
This research is grounded in the sociology of mobility, which shifts the analytical focus from mere movement used called mobility but in reality it's only taking into account the viscosity aspect and not the underlying capacity for movement (motility) and the social practices that shape it. This framework is essential for understanding the gap between the provision of transport infrastructure and its actual use.

\subsection{The Concept of Motility}

Motility is defined as the capacity of an individual or a group to be mobile in social and geographic space \cite{kaufmann_motility_2004}. It is not the act of moving itself, but the potential for movement. This potential is structured by three interdependent elements:

    Access: This refers to the available mobility tools and opportunities within a specific context. It includes the physical transport infrastructure (e.g., roads, cycle paths, rail networks), the available services (e.g., timetables, pricing schemes), and the legal and institutional frameworks (e.g., driving licenses, residency permits). Access is the objective component of the system.

    Skills: These are the individual competencies required to utilize access. They include not only physical skills (e.g., the ability to cycle or drive) but also cognitive skills (e.g., reading a network map, navigating a ticket machine) and organizational skills (e.g., trip chaining, time management).

    Cognitive Appropriation: This is the most subjective and crucial element. It encompasses how individuals perceive, evaluate, and assign meaning to the access and skills available to them. It includes personal preferences, habits, values (e.g., environmental consciousness), perceptions of safety, and the social meanings attached to different modes of transport (e.g., the status of a car).

Motility is, therefore, the product of the interaction between an individual's skills and cognitive appropriation with the access provided by their environment.

\subsection{Spatial and Temporal Dimensions of Mobility}

The motility framework allows for a refined understanding of spatial and temporal constraints.

    Spatial Viscosity: This concept describes the level of friction or resistance encountered when moving through space. A environment with high viscosity (e.g., a car-dependent suburb with poor public transport) requires significant effort, time, or resources to traverse. In contrast, a low-viscosity environment (e.g., a dense, mixed-use neighborhood) facilitates easy movement. Viscosity is not absolute; it is experienced differently based on an individual's motility.

    Travel Time vs. Perceived Time: A central distinction for this study is between objectively measured travel time (the time calculated by a model or a clock) and subjectively perceived travel time. Perceived time is influenced by factors such as the activity performed while traveling (e.g., working on a train vs. concentrating on traffic), the comfort and stress of the journey, and a sense of control or predictability. A 30-minute train ride spent reading may be perceived as shorter and more useful than a 25-minute drive in congested traffic.

\subsection{Synthesis: Implications for Spatial Planning}

This conceptual framework provides the lens through which Swiss planning documents will be analyzed. The key question is whether these documents operate with a simplistic model of mobility as movement, focusing primarily on access (e.g., building infrastructure) and objective travel time. Or, do they engage with the more complex, sociological model of motility, which would involve considering how policies affect skills and cognitive appropriation, and acknowledge the role of perceived time and spatial viscosity in shaping modal choice? The presence or absence of this more nuanced vocabulary and rationale will be a key indicator of the human-centricity of planning policy.
