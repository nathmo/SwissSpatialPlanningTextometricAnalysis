\section{Conceptual Framework \& Key Definitions}
This research is grounded in the sociology of mobility, which shifts the analytical focus from the mere movement by taking into account the individual.
It's important to define and clarify a few concept first. The first one is Motility and the second one is Accessibility. In the general knowledge, mobility only take into account the Accessibility aspect and not the capacity for movement nor the social practices that shape it. This framework is essential for understanding the gap between the provision of transport infrastructure and its actual use. Motility regroup the accessibility and the user's skill and motivation to use that accessibility and together theses three notion become mobility.

\subsection{The Concept of Motility}

Motility is defined as the capacity of an individual or a group to be mobile in social and geographic space \cite{kaufmann_motility_2004}. It is not the act of moving itself, but the potential for movement. This potential is structured by three interdependent elements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Access:} This refers to the available mobility tools and opportunities within a specific context. It includes the physical transport infrastructure (e.g., roads, cycle paths, rail networks), the available services (e.g., timetables, pricing schemes, owning a vehicle), and the legal and institutional frameworks (e.g., driving licenses, residency permits). Access is the objective component of the system.

    \item \textbf{Skills:} These are the individual competencies required to utilize access. They include not only physical skills (e.g., the ability to cycle or drive) but also cognitive skills (e.g., reading a network map, use a ticket machine) and organizational skills (e.g., time management, trip optimization).

    \item \textbf{Cognitive Appropriation:} This is the most subjective and crucial element. It encompasses how individuals perceive, evaluate, and assign meaning to the access and skills available to them. It includes personal preferences, habits, values (e.g., environmental consciousness), perceptions of safety, and the social meanings attached to different modes of transport (e.g., the status of a car). To give a practical example, someone might prefer taking the train due to being able to re-appropriate the travel time by socializing with friends or doing administrative tasks.
\end{itemize}

Motility is, therefore, the product of the interaction between an individual's skills, the cognitive appropriation and the individual access.

\subsection{Accessibility and the Institutional Perspective on Mobility}

Accessibility represents the degree to which places, services, and opportunities can be reached within a given spatial and temporal context. Within the motility framework, accessibility corresponds to the \textit{access} dimension: the tangible opportunities for movement available to an individual. It determines what movements are possible in principle, while skills and cognitive appropriation determine which of these possibilities become actual mobility practices.

In everyday discourse and most planning documents, however, mobility tends to be reduced to this accessibility dimension. When policymakers or engineers discuss “improving mobility,” they often mean improving accessibility: reducing travel times, increasing network coverage, or enhancing service frequency. This reduction overlooks the skills, perceptions, and motivation that mediate how people actually use mobility systems. As a result, there can be a gap between the provision of infrastructure and its effective use.

From an institutional perspective, accessibility is usually assessed through measurable indicators. These include physical and temporal metrics such as travel time, cost, and service frequency (“qualité de la desserte”), but also considerations (since quite recently) of inclusivity such as the accessibility of transport for people with disabilities, affordability, and multimodal connectivity. These factors together define the “objective” structure of mobility opportunities in a given territory.

Yet, while accessibility can describe how well-connected a place is in physical terms, it cannot alone account for how people interpret and appropriate those connections. For example, two neighborhoods might have identical public transport coverage, but their residents may display very different travel behaviors due to differences in skills, habits, or perceptions of comfort and safety.

In this sense, accessibility forms the structural foundation of motility. As a mathematician would describe it : necessary but not sufficient for mobility to occur. Understanding this distinction is key to developing human-centered mobility policies that engage with how people experience, value, and enact movement in their daily lives and thus maximize the effectiveness of the measure taken.

\subsection{Synthesis: Implications for Spatial Planning}

This conceptual framework provides the lens through which Swiss planning documents will be analyzed. The key question is whether these documents operate with a simplistic model of mobility as movement, focusing primarily on access (e.g., building infrastructure) and objective travel time. Or, do they engage with the more complex, sociological model of motility and viscosity, which would involve considering how policies affect skills and cognitive appropriation, and acknowledge the role of perceived time in shaping modal choice? The presence or absence of this more nuanced vocabulary and rationale will be a key indicator of the human-centricity of planning policy.
