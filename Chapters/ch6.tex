\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Limitations of the Keyword Approach}

The first limitation of this study concerns the use of keyword frequency as a proxy for conceptual presence. The textometric approach offers a reproducible way to measure linguistic patterns but counting words alone cannot ensure that they refer to the same idea across contexts. A high frequency of a term does not always mean that the underlying concept is strongly represented.

Future work should attempt at addressing this issue. This would strengthen the link between observed data and theoretical concepts. More advanced computational methods, such as contextual embeddings or supervised models, could help by taking into account how meaning shifts across contexts.

\subsection{Challenges in Modeling Motility}

A second limitation relates to how motility is modeled. Currently, there is no shared quantitative model of motility. The framework proposed by Kaufmann and later authors remains mainly qualitative, with the exception of accessibility, focused on the interaction between access, skills, and cognitive appropriation. Turning these elements into measurable indicators is a challenge.

Even if a complete model existed, combining the different dimensions into one global indicator would be problematic. From a philosophical point of view, there is no neutral way to collapse complex human phenomena into a single number. Each weighting reflects implicit values. For that reason, a multi-criteria approach such as Pareto fronts may be viable. It identifies solutions that are not dominated by others without imposing a single hierarchy. But it can only help to weed out the solution that are equal or inferior on every metrics. Since most solutions are tradeoff, they tend to be better in one metrics at the expense of another which Pareto front cannot solve. The final choice then remains with human decision-makers, who must accept the responsibility and bias that come with judgment.

\subsection{Implications for Swiss Spatial Planning}

The results suggest that Swiss spatial planning discourse still gives more attention to accessibility in its physical and infrastructural sense than to the human factors of mobility. Cantonal master plans mostly focus on network coverage, travel time, and service quality. They rarely address the motivation or skills that allow people to use these systems effectively. This shows that planning practice remains centered on supply-side measures, while the demand-side, related to behavior and perception, is often neglected.

Integrating motility into planning would mean designing measures that develop not only access but also the ability and willingness to use sustainable transport. This could involve highlighting the personal benefits of public transport and active mobility, and creating conditions that make their use natural and rewarding in everyday life.

\subsection{Addressing the under-representation of Motivation and Skills}

The under-representation of the “motivation” and “skills” dimensions of motility can be partly mitigated through targeted interventions. Measures such as training programs for using public or active transport, and introductory mobility workshops can help build both competence and confidence and help shape new habits. free or reduced-cost access to public transport could also help by both helping the accessibility of public transport and the motivations of the users. Interventions during key life stages, for example in schools, could encourage lasting habits of sustainable travel.

Existing research in “mobility management”\cite{noauthor_impact_nodate} \cite{moser_effectiveness_2008} shows that soft incentive to limit car use exist but their effect is debated\cite{wall_response_2011}. Kaufmann's approach could help improve the effectiveness of such measure by addressing the reason why people find that car are a better tradeoff from their perspective, and thus offer more effective measure that feel less frustrating to the user.

Further work is needed to understand how these ideas could be applied systematically within Swiss planning. Operationalizing motility as a concrete planning objective remains an open challenge, both theoretical and practical.
